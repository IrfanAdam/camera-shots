01 — Vision

Cinema is a language, not a lottery.

Every great shot answers a question before it fills a frame: whose eyes are we in, how close do we stand, what moves and what holds still. The product treats those answers as the work itself.

The wiki teaches shot language — size, framing, angle, movement, light, composition the way directors actually use it. The engine turns that language into structure: scenes, shots, locations, props, cast, composed into reusable prompt tokens. The thesis is simple — deliberate intent in, deliberate cinema out.

Direct with intent: every shot has a job before it has a look.

— the working rule behind both tools

02 — Problem

Prompt roulette is not directing.

Type a paragraph, get four random clips, repeat until something looks right. That is the current workflow — and it fails exactly where cinema matters most.

Prompt roulette

Long prose prompts with no shot grammar. Every run is a fresh gamble; nothing learned carries forward.

Style drift

Shot three forgets shot one. Focal length, light, and palette wander because nothing pins them down.

Weak shot language

Vague verbs — "cinematic, dramatic" — instead of named decisions: close-up or wide, dolly or lock, key from the side or golden backlight.

No continuity

Screen direction flips, eyelines break, coverage never gets planned. The edit inherits the mess.

03 — The Craft Gap

Between taste and tools, nobody teaches the middle.

Filmmakers know what good looks like. Generators make pixels fast. What is missing is the craft layer: naming the shot, justifying it, and holding it across a sequence.

  • Knowledge is scattered. Shot craft lives across textbooks, essays, and long video series — rarely in one working reference.
  • Planning has no home. Shot lists live in docs, stills in folders, tokens in chat logs. Nothing connects the beat to the phrase that describes it.
  • Prompts are not reusable. Good phrasing gets typed once and lost. Tokens like CU — close-up, face fills the frame should be copy-ready, every time.

04 — How It Works

Learn it. Board it. Compose it.

Three moves, each with its own surface — no accounts, no rendering, no model lock-in. The output is deliberate language you paste wherever you generate.

1. Learn the language — the wiki

Shot size, framing, angle, movement, lens, light, composition: what each choice does for the story, when to use it, and its token (CU, DOLLY, GOLD) ready to copy.

2. Compose in the engine — the engine

Storyboard scenes and shots; track locations, props, and cast. Every card composes a prompt preview with its anchor first — continuity built in, not bolted on.

3. Export deliberate intent

Copy a stub — SHOT-XX — phrase — and paste it into any generator, brief, or shot list. The craft travels; the tool stays out of the way.

05 — Built For

People who care about the frame.

Not for chasing trends — for anyone who wants AI footage to look decided rather than accidental.

Directors

Block beats, name intent, and hand off coverage that actually cuts together.

Cinematographers

Pin lens, angle, movement, and light per shot — and hold them across the sequence.

AI filmmakers

Replace prose roulette with composed tokens: composition, rhythm, and continuity you can repeat.

Students of craft

Learn why a close-up lands, when a dolly beats a zoom, and how screen direction holds.

06 — Principles

What the work refuses to do.

  • Intent before pixels. Name the job of the shot before choosing its look.
  • Tokens over prose. Short, named, reusable phrases beat paragraphs that vanish.
  • Structure over generation. A storyboarded sequence with anchors beats four lucky clips. The product generates nothing — on purpose.
  • Craft is portable. What you learn and compose here works in any generator, brief, or edit.

07 — What's Next

Deepen what already exists.

No new promises — the near-term direction is making the current loop sharper: richer language, deeper composing, and a tighter connection between the two.

  • Now — deepen the wiki's shot language. More entries, sharper when-to-use guidance, and copy-ready tokens that stay consistent.
  • Next — more storyboard and composer depth in the engine. Richer shot and sequence composing, so anchors and continuity hold across scenes.
  • Later — tighten the wiki → engine → intent loop. Make learning a term and using it in a board feel like one motion, so craft carries straight into the prompt.

Principles

Four rules the build obeys.

Intent before pixels.

Why: every shot must declare its job before its look, or continuity has nothing to hold.

Tokens over prose.

Why: named reusable phrases survive across runs; paragraphs vanish after one copy.

Structure over generation.

Why: the product generates nothing, so it can never drift, bill, or break on a model change.

Craft is portable.

Why: plain-text stubs work in any generator, brief, or edit — no lock-in, no export tax.

Architecture

Four layers, entries to export.

01 — Entries

Shot-language wiki pages: size, angle, movement, lens, light, composition — each with when-to-use guidance.

02 — Tokens

Every entry emits a copy-ready token (CU, DOLLY, GOLD) with versions, so phrasing improves without breaking boards.

03 — Compose / reasoning

Scenes break into shots; anchors (lens, light, palette, screen direction) are pinned first and held across the sequence — this is where intent becomes structure.

04 — Export

Model-agnostic plain-text stubs (SHOT-XX — phrase) that paste into any generator, brief, or shot list.

Security

Local-first means little to attack.

  • Local-first data. Boards live in the user's browser localStorage — nothing to breach server-side because there is no server side.
  • No accounts, no secrets. No passwords, tokens, or payment data exist anywhere in the system.
  • No vendor promises. No third-party model, analytics, or tracking dependency is claimed or required.

Scope

In, out, and when out comes back.

In scope

Wiki entries linked to prompt fields · versioned token library · scene→shot locked templates · continuity ledger · one-action-per-shot checker · model-agnostic export.

Out of scope

No model training — re-entry: never, product generates nothing. No rendering pipeline — re-entry: only via export stubs. No accounts/billing — re-entry: when teams need sync.

Requirements

FR-01 to FR-06, each with a done-when gate.

FR-01 — Wiki-linked prompt fields

Every wiki entry maps to generatable prompt fields.

Done when: each entry renders its fields + copy token.

FR-02 — Versioned token library

Tokens carry versions; phrasing improves without breaking boards.

Done when: token edit creates a version; old boards pin the old one.

FR-03 — Scene→shot locked templates

Beats break into shots with anchors pinned at creation.

Done when: new shot inherits scene anchors, locked by default.

FR-04 — Continuity ledger

Lens, light, palette, screen direction held across the sequence.

Done when: changing a ledger value flags every affected shot.

FR-05 — One-action-per-shot checker

Flags shots trying to do two jobs at once.

Done when: two-verb shots warn with a one-click split.

FR-06 — Model-agnostic export

Plain-text stubs paste into any generator.

Done when: board exports to clipboard as SHOT-XX stubs with zero lab-specific syntax.

Arch principles

How the build stays small.

  • Phase-gated: each backlog phase starts only when the prior gate passes.
  • Single-adapter first: one ingest (wiki terms) → one timeline (shot board) → one artifact (text stub).
  • Scenario, not forecast: all money math is labelled scenario-model, never commitment.
  • Static until sync: no backend until teams demand cross-device boards.
